Customary ownership accounts for 80 per cent of Ghana land, Lands Commission says

Disagreements over family leadership and double sales of plots continue to create risk for home buyers and developers across the country.

Sweet Melodies News
WhatsAppX Facebook

Customary land accounts for eighty per cent of Ghana’s total land area, leaving public property at twenty per cent, a Lands Commission official told Citi FM.

Why it matters

Families and churches buying land for homes or places of worship face risk due to unapproved sales and missing roads. Establishing who holds genuine title to traditional property remains essential before making payments.

The details

Speaking on the Accra-based radio station, Deputy Executive Secretary in charge of Operations Raphael Hokey outlined major obstacles facing land administration across the nation. Adom News reported his remarks.

The land ownership structure affects families, churches, workers and traders seeking to purchase plots for homes, farms or commercial space across Ghana. Prospective buyers frequently encounter unapproved sales and unclear boundaries when trying to secure property.

Mr Hokey explained that public lands comprise state lands and vested lands. For vested lands, the state holds legal interest, but customary owners retain beneficial interest, creating management complexities.

According to Adom News, Mr Hokey noted that management bodies face constraints because they attempt to manage land they do not own or properly control. He said family disagreements over leadership authority regularly cause legal battles.

Another persistent problem is the practice of selling a single parcel to multiple buyers. MyJoyOnline reported that these double sales create deep uncertainty for prospective landowners across the country.

Mr Hokey also raised concern about landowners selling plots without offering access roads, basic utilities or clear boundaries. Buyers are left uncertain whether landowning families or the state should provide basic infrastructure.

To enforce regulations, Mr Hokey said local assemblies could work with the police service to address unauthorized developments. He added that assemblies may seek court orders to resolve complex cases.

What’s next

Local assemblies are expected to work with the police service and courts to halt unapproved construction and enforce layout regulations across the regions.
